# Decisions

Material design and architecture decisions, with the reason for each. Newest last within each section.

## Stack

- **Phaser 4 + its bundled Matter.js, TypeScript, Vite, Vitest, Playwright.** Phaser gives a fast WebGL 2D renderer and camera; Matter is the rigid-body engine Phaser already ships, so there is one physics build for the browser and for headless tests (imported through the `follyworks-matter` alias, see `src/sim/matter.ts`).
- **No external art or audio files.** All part art, environments, particles and sound are generated procedurally at runtime (`src/render/art/*`, `src/audio/*`). The texture bank (`src/render/TextureBank.ts`) is the replacement boundary if painted assets arrive later.
- **Fonts are bundled via `@fontsource`** so the game works offline.

## Simulation

- **The simulation is pure and deterministic at a fixed 60 Hz tick.** It knows nothing about Phaser. RUN builds a fresh `Simulation` from (level, build); RESET throws it away. That gives exact reset, reproducible runs, and headless level tests that use the very same code as the game.
- **Rewind/scrub by snapshots** (`src/sim/history.ts`): a full snapshot every 2 ticks, capped at 2700 entries (90 s of run time). Scrubbing restores a snapshot for display.
- **Resuming after a rewind replays from scratch instead of from the snapshot.** Matter keeps solver state (contact caches, warm-start impulses) that a snapshot cannot hold, so a restored world drifts from the original within a second or two and "rewind, resume" could change the outcome. `RunController.resync()` builds a fresh simulation and fast-forwards it to the scrubbed tick before stepping on. Ticks cost 30–90 µs, so even a 20 s fast-forward is imperceptible, and the replay is exact.
- **Two Matter substeps per tick.** Without them a bowling ball dropped from ceiling height (~18 px per tick) tunnels through a 12 px plank or seesaw. Component forces are re-applied for the second substep because Matter clears them after each update.
- **Round bodies use real disc inertia; dominoes use real rectangle inertia.** Matter inflates inertia 4x for stacking stability. On a ball that meant two thirds of its speed turned into spin the moment it touched the floor, so balls stopped after half a metre. Round bodies are built as 32+ sided polygons for the same reason (Matter otherwise caps a 14 px ball at 14 sides).
- **Gentle contacts never bounce, and buckets absorb bounces.** `Pair.update` is wrapped once so contacts approaching slower than 1.2 px/tick get zero restitution (rolling balls stop chattering on their facets) and bodies flagged `plugin.absorb` (the bucket) use the less bouncy of the two materials instead of Matter's default "bounciest wins", so rubber balls stay in buckets.
- **A contact that starts and ends inside one tick still counts.** With two substeps, a fast ball can touch a trampoline and leave it before the end-of-tick contact list is built, and the trampoline would act like a plain block, depending on sub-pixel placement. New contacts are also noted after the first substep and reported if they vanished by the end of the tick.
- **Impact speed is measured from pre-step velocities.** By the time contacts are reported the solver has already resolved the bounce, so impact sounds, trampolines and fragile parts read the velocity captured at the start of the tick.
- **Domino assist.** A domino tilted past ~11° that touches a standing neighbour on its falling side hands on its spin (at least 0.035 rad/tick) and pivots the neighbour about its foot. Pure rigid-body contact shoved neighbours sideways or let the chain stall leaning; players expect dominoes to just work.
- **Seesaw ends have small lips** so a ball parked on the low end waits to be launched instead of rolling off as soon as the plank tilts.
- **Bolt (the robot) steps over low kerbs and brakes when unpowered.** He climbs anything no taller than his ankles that has free space above it (a plank lying on the floor, a ramp's end, ramps up to ~11°) and still turns around at real walls. His feet have no friction (so he walks smoothly), so he now brakes explicitly when power is cut instead of skating on for a metre.
- **Light is a raycast, recomputed every tick and on restore.** Beams are pure geometry from the current world, so they are never stored in snapshots and rewind stays exact. Optical parts are ordinary static bodies tagged `plugin.optic`; anything else that is solid and not a sensor blocks a beam (mounted parts with collision mask 0 are see-through).
- **Mirrors are silvered on both long faces; their end caps absorb.** Two-sided mirrors forgive placing a part the "wrong" way round, which is the common beginner mistake, and still make angle matter. Hitting a short edge (more than about 37° off a face) stops the beam so a mirror cannot be used edge-on as a wall-piercing trick.
- **Prisms use fixed, exaggerated deviations** (red 15°, green 22.5°, blue 30°, toward the base) from the exit point instead of Snell's law, so a fan is wide enough to aim at and the numbers are readable in hints. Lenses are ideal thin lenses: a ray at any height goes through the point on the focal plane that a parallel ray through the centre would reach.
- **Beams heat after a 0.25 s dwell**, scaled by summed intensity and decaying when unlit, so a beam sweeping past a candle does not light it but one resting on it does. Ropes burn through in about 0.6 s.
- **Light sensors already lit when the machine is built start lit.** Otherwise every NOT-gate tripwire would fire once at t=0 before the first trace. The sensor's chain stage still counts on its first lit tick. The laser reuses the magnet's hum loop rather than adding a new audio asset.

## Rules

- **Wires are free; ropes and belts come from the parts bin.** Wiring is about logic, not scarcity.
- **A player may not add a wire into an input socket the level already feeds.** Otherwise a pre-wired battery could be run straight to the device and skip the level's switch. Wiring a level's battery output to more things is allowed (tutorial 5 depends on wiring two level parts together).
- **ELEGANT is earned by meeting either authored target: few parts (`elegantParts`) or a quick finish (`elegantTime`).** The spec frames elegance as "economy, fewer components, or efficient completion", and levels are small enough that "either" can't be gamed by a huge fast machine. The briefing shows both targets.
- **ABSURD counts distinct stages of the chain reaction** (each part's first activation, including two loose things knocking into each other) up to the moment of solving. The default target is 7, with a per-level override `bonus.absurdStages`.
- **Every campaign level has a hand-tuned ABSURD target, set just above its simplest solution and proven by an authored over-the-top build.** A flat 7 turned out to be unreachable on most levels (verified solutions reached 0–6 stages). `tests/levels/absurd.test.ts` checks that the simplest solution misses ABSURD and the elaborate one earns it.
- **Tutorials have no ABSURD bonus (`absurdStages: 0`).** Their parts bins are too small for any build to beat the plain solution, so showing a target there would only advertise something impossible.
- **Level walls can be as thin as 6 px** (the minimum was 20), so authors can build chutes, lips and racks without fat masonry.
- **Gears may overlap the hub of any fixed, non-moving part that has a rotor** (a motor, a pulley, another gear's axle). Meshing needs the rims to touch, which the overlap rule otherwise forbade.
- **A stalled run says so early.** When a level with goals has had nothing moving, burning, counting down or part-way through a hold goal for 2 s, a banner offers "Back to building" instead of making the player wait out the time limit. It is a read-only check, so it cannot change a run, and every reference solution is tested never to trigger it before solving.
- **Parts got bigger by making the room smaller, not by rescaling physics.** Every campaign level, the sandbox and new custom levels use a 1120×630 room (`STANDARD_WORLD`, was 1600×900) and the camera crops tighter around it, so parts show about 1.5× bigger. Scaling the parts themselves would have meant retuning gravity, forces, the domino assist, robot kerb heights and every level; shrinking the room kept all tuned behaviour and the physics tests intact. Old imported levels keep whatever size they declare.
- **The campaign is a short tutorial plus mission groups of about ten.** Six guided tutorial missions, then five groups (Workshop Basics, Levers & Lines, Moving Parts, Hot Air & Sparks, Ridiculous Machines). Within each group missions start with more machinery and need more parts; `tests/levels/campaign.test.ts` measures both (non-wall starting objects, and parts in the simplest reference solution) and fails if a group stops ramping. Adding a group is a new file in `src/game/levels/` and a row in `CHAPTERS`.
- **Moving Parts missions guard their shortcuts with a second goal instead of build zones.** With no build zones, a loose plank or domino could often reach the last goal directly (pins knocked by dominoes, a cat woken by a dropped toaster). Goals such as "Bolt reaches the lane", "the bowling ball reaches the breakfast shelf" or "ring the bell with toast" make the intended chain load-bearing, and each mission lists the shortcut as a counterexample. Where a throw has to land precisely, the mission uses a bowling ball, a bank shot off a backboard, or a guard wall to catch strays, because light bouncy balls scattered too much under the ±4 px nudge test.
- **Hot Air & Sparks chains never hand the player early power or an easy kill.** Players may wire any level output to their own parts, so the group 4 machines feed switches, mats and toasters from the player's battery or from a level output that only fires late in the chain, never from a level battery that could skip the chain. A balloon that floats out of the room also counts as `destroyed`, so balloon-popping goals keep every balloon caged under a ceiling or shelf.
- **"Lift X into the bell" is a height goal, not a contact goal.** A `contact` goal only sees Matter's active pairs, so a crate that clips a bell and rests against it can ring the bell (the brief hit still fires) while the goal never sees the touch. That failed for some of the ±4 px nudged runs. Where that bites (Grand Opening), use a `height` goal with `maxY` at the bell instead: it reads the same to the player and holds reliably. Missions where the crate rams the bell squarely (Counter Culture, Two Birds) keep the contact goal and pass every nudge.
- **Ridiculous Machines gates the player with prebuilt wiring.** Wires are free, so a mission that leaves an input unwired can be shortcut straight from a battery. Group 5 feeds every key input (toasters, robots, gates) from a level-owned plate or switch, so the only way in is to make that plate press; goals that need several objects of one type use one goal per id, because `enterRegion` on a type selector passes with a single object.
- **Tutorial guidance is data and never restricts the player.** A level may carry `guide` steps (text, an optional pointer at a bin entry, HUD control or spot in the room, an optional ghost outline of a part, and a trigger: place, connect, run or "got it"). The current step is simply the first one whose trigger isn't met, so players who already know what to do skip ahead, and removing a part steps back. It can be hidden from the card, the top bar or Settings.
- **Tutorial and Workshop Basics goals are goofy outcomes, not "get X to the zone".** Tutorial missions swish a hoop, ring a bell, knock pins or wake the cat, each with a little starting contraption to watch, while still teaching one control and needing 0 to 3 parts. Group 1 uses only gravity-and-collision parts plus the cartoon set (bells, hoops, pins, cats, chickens, mousetraps, toasters), with parts needed rising from 2 to 6 and starting objects from 5 to 17. Chaotic links such as trampoline tosses and seesaw launches always land in a wide funnel or tray, so the ±4 px jitter check passes, and the cat is turned by a plank stood on end rather than by a gap, because the cat leaps gaps of 50 px or less.
- **The Physics Lab is its own list, not part of the campaign.** Lessons are self-contained and all open, while the campaign tests require difficulty to ramp. Each lesson has counterexample builds that try the wrong idea (three rubber balls instead of one bowling ball; a crate with no counterweight ball) and are tested to fail. The per-level checks are shared through `tests/levels/entryChecks.ts`, a copy of the campaign's, so `campaign.test.ts` stays untouched.
- **No friction-angle or trampoline lesson.** In the engine a crate creeps down planks well below its sliding angle and starts sliding at shallow angles after a knock, so a "find the angle where it slides" lesson would teach the engine rather than friction. The trampoline guarantees a minimum upward kick (more energy out than in), which is not how a real spring behaves. Both cards say this in their "In the workshop" line instead.
- **Science cards must be true.** Each card is 2–4 sentences, and every game simplification (fixed pulleys only, motors that never run out of strength, the domino assist, single-wire circuits, the bolted-down cannon) is stated in its `inGame` line rather than glossed over. "Physics in your machine" comes from the run's chain stages, so it lists only what actually happened.
- **Campaign unlocking is soft:** a level is open if it is within 3 of the number solved, already solved, or "unlock all" is on in settings. Nobody gets stuck behind one puzzle.
- **Easy and Hard are derived, never authored.** `applyDifficulty(entry, d)` (`game/difficulty.ts`) builds them from the level plus `solutions[0]`, so every mission, including groups added later, gets all three and `tests/levels/difficulty.test.ts` proves each one solvable. Normal is the level exactly as authored.
  - *Easy:* time limit ×1.5 (rounded up); +1 of every part type `solutions[0]` uses (unlimited stays unlimited); a part cap rises by one; and when `solutions[0]` places two or more objects, one of them (the first that no solution connection touches, else the first) is pre-placed at its exact spot as a locked starting object. The bin is not reduced for that gift, so Easy's bin is a superset of Normal's. Missions whose solution is a single part get no pre-placed part, because that would leave nothing to do.
  - *Hard:* time limit cut toward 60% of Normal but never below the slowest of `solutions[0]` and its ±4 px nudges ×1.3 + 2 s (measured headlessly on first use and memoised; about 0.1 s), and never above Normal; types `solutions[0]` uses are trimmed to exactly its counts and every other type (the decoys) to at most one, so Hard does not reveal which parts matter; the part cap is `solutions[0]`'s part count. Hard switches ABSURD off: with the cap at the simplest solution's size it is out of reach, and the briefing must not advertise the impossible.
  - Progress is recorded per difficulty (`LevelProgress.byDifficulty`) and the old top-level fields stay as the aggregate, so a solve on any difficulty unlocks the next missions. Saves from before difficulties count as Normal. Builds autosave per difficulty (`<id>@easy`, `<id>@hard`; Normal keeps the plain id), so a Normal machine never lands in a Hard bin it no longer fits. The choice lives in settings.
- **Difficulty is chosen once and sticks.** Asking in every briefing made players re-decide on every mission ("KISS for the end user"). The first campaign mission shows one full-screen choice with Normal recommended; after that every mission uses `settings.difficulty` until it is changed in Settings, and the briefing, HUD and results only show a read-only badge that links there. A change made while a mission is open does not swap the level under the player (their build autosaves per difficulty); it applies the next time a mission opens, and Settings offers "Restart it on …" for the open one. Saves that already remembered a difficulty from the old briefing picker count as chosen, so returning players are never asked.
- **Hints are a ladder, and only ghosts cost anything.** Each press climbs one step: the authored hint lines (a nudge), then "parts you'll need" from the reference solution, then one ghost outline of a reference part at its exact spot per press, skipping parts the player has already put there (and Easy's pre-placed part). Stars are never taken away and SOLVED/ABSURD are unaffected, but once a ghost has been shown ELEGANT is withheld for that session's solves: an outline of the intended machine is the solution, and elegance should mean you found it. Nudges and the parts list keep ELEGANT. The results card says "Solved with hints" whenever any tier was used, and progress records whether each difficulty has a hint-free solve. Custom levels have no reference solution, so their ladder stops at the authored nudges. Hints shown automatically on entering a tutorial do not count.

- **Noise is a one-tick broadcast, not a body.** `sim.noise` records a circle; after `applyHeat` each tick every live part within it gets `onNoise`. Listeners are pure state changes, so rewind needs nothing extra; `restore()` just clears the list. Radii are per part (bell 320, chicken 260, explosions twice the blast) so earshot is a tuning knob, not a simulation of sound.
- **Bowling pins get a helping hand, like dominoes.** Plain rigid pins slid along the floor as one block or wedged against each other. A knock from something loose tips a pin away from the hitter about its base, a heavy hard hit launches it, and past its tipping point (22°) a pin keeps rotating, so a pin leaning on a standing neighbour shoves it over. "Down" is a tilt over 50°. A ball into six pins knocks down five or six across the spacings and speeds we tried.
- **The mousetrap sets the launch velocity, scaled by mass^-0.25.** Adding to the current velocity made the arc depend on how things landed; dividing by mass made a crate barely hop. A quarter power keeps a crate and a chicken both clearing a low wall, the same way every time.
- **Basket counts come from a `tally`, with no hold time.** A swish is the instant a centre crosses the rim line downward between the posts, so the ball is long gone by the time a held count would finish. The hoop keeps the ids it has counted in `e.state.swished` (each thing counts once), and `containerCount` reads that instead of `interior`.
- **Keyed chain stages (`pot:boil`, `hoop>ball`) caption at the part they name.** Dynamic parts become a stage on their first bump ("Cat hit the floor"), so the goofy parts use their own keys for their real moment; the floating label now finds the entity before the `:`/`>` instead of appearing at the top-left corner (which also fixed the magnet and KABOOM captions).
- **Lasers & Light tripwires arm themselves.** Only always-on lasers light sensors before RUN, so a battery-powered tripwire leaves its NOT gate on for the first tick and would fire at once. Tripwire Toast latches the sensor's first light (a timer with delay 0) and ANDs it with the NOT, so the alarm goes off only when a lit beam is broken, and an unfiltered beam never arms it, which keeps the filter load-bearing.

## Rendering

- **The renderer interpolates between ticks** using the pose captured at the start of each step, and measures frame time itself with `performance.now()` (Phaser's smoothed delta under-reports at low frame rates, which made runs play in slow motion).
- **Environment art is three layers** (far, light, near). The near layer is a parallax foreground restricted to the margins so it never hides machine parts.
- **Floating stage labels scale with 1/zoom** so they stay readable at any camera zoom.
- **Phaser renders with `maxTextures: 1`.** With four or more textures batched together, the software WebGL used in testing (SwiftShader) drew some quads torn in half (a plank as a thin wedge). One texture per batch costs a few extra draw calls, which a scene of this size does not notice. `?maxtex=N` overrides it for comparison.
- **The near foreground layer is cleared over the whole world rectangle**, not just its middle, after a pipe in the maintenance room hid a battery.
- **Room dressing is knocked back so it can't be mistaken for parts.** Environment props are painted on their own layer and composited with lower saturation, brightness and contrast plus a slight blur. Every part gets a dark rim (`TextureBank.getRim`) drawn under its painted layers, and connection sockets have a cream outer ring, so parts and connection points read against any wall.
- **Rotate and resize happen on the part itself.** The selected part shows a knob on a stem above it (drag to turn it, snapping to the part's own step, Alt for 1°) and, when it has a size, square grips on its ends. Dragging a grip keeps the opposite end where it was. The preview edits the document object live and is put back before one `reshapeObject` commit, so a whole drag is one undo step; a shape that would overlap something is refused on release, the same rule as moving. The properties panel's size sliders now get the same overlap check (they used to allow overlaps).
- **Long parts are edited like lines: grab an end and swing it.** The end grips of turnable long parts (plank, conveyor, a wall's long axis) move that end freely; the other end is the pivot and the part turns and stretches to follow, snapping angle (the part's own step, 5° if none) and length (the prop's step), with Alt for free angles and Shift to keep the length. This is the gesture players know from line tools in Figma and Keynote, and it builds a ramp from point A to point B in one drag instead of rotate-then-resize. A seesaw cannot turn, so its grips only stretch (square grips mean stretch, round grips mean swing). Corners turn the part about its centre from an invisible zone just outside each corner (never on a part, so grabbing a part still moves it); the knob stays for touch, where nothing can be hovered. The geometry is pure (`game/manipulation.ts`) and unit-tested; every gesture still previews on the document and commits once through `reshapeObject`, refused on release if it overlaps.
- **The selection toolbar floats beside the part, never on it or the HUD.** It tries above (clearing the knob), below, right, then left, prefers a spot that leaves neighbouring parts visible, stays inside the stage area the HUD insets leave free, and hides while dragging, placing, using a tool and running. It turns by at least 15° (a part with a coarser own step uses that), so two presses make a clean 30° ramp. `R` now straightens (it used to be a second key for "turn right", which `E` already is).
- **Only parts whose bodies are built from a size get resize grips** (plank, seesaw, conveyor, and walls in the level editor), declared with `resize` on the component. Resizing changes the prop the body is built from, so physics stays exact. Balls, crates, robots and the rest are not scalable: scaling them would change mass, the domino assist, robot kerb heights and every tuned level (see "Parts got bigger by making the room smaller").
- **Goal tags dodge the space a mission needs.** A tag points at its zone or part from one of a few nearby spots (above, slid sideways, below, beside, stacked) and takes the one that covers least of the reference solution's part spots, the parts in the room, other tags and the view edge (`game/tagPlacement.ts`, pure and checked for every mission and lesson in `tests/levels/goalTags.test.ts`). Spots are chosen in build mode and kept during a run so tags do not dance, and tags fade while a part is dragged or placed. John found a tag sitting on the exact spot a part had to go.
- **Goals are numbered and tagged in the room.** Each top-bar chip has a number, a type icon and a live detail (container count, hold timer), and each goal gets a matching DOM tag next to its zone, container or target that follows it every frame (`ui/GoalMarkers.ts`, text from `goalMarker` in `sim/goals.ts`). Tags for goals that share a spot stack instead of hiding each other. Pointing at a chip highlights its tag and zone. In build mode a dashed arc runs from a named target to its goal zone.
- **Themes reskin parts at paint time instead of shipping six sets of part art.** Each of 31 painters stays the single source; `render/skin.ts` grades its canvas per theme. Alpha is never changed, so silhouettes, sockets and hit areas match in every theme, and physics is not involved at all. The cost is one extra pixel pass per texture the first time it is painted in a theme; textures from the previous theme are released on a switch.
- **Retro and Comic posterize a slightly blurred copy.** Posterizing the raw art turned painted grain into noisy blotches; a small alpha-weighted blur first gives flat fills, and the ink outline comes from a distance transform of the alpha.
- **Themed rooms are new environments, not filters on the old ones**, because a filter cannot turn a garage into a cave. Modern keeps each level's own room. Theme art is original: the retro look is a generic 90s toolbox (no assets or names from any game), and the comic look is an invented city and caption style (no publisher characters or names).
- **The theme setting defaults to "Auto (follows the era)"**, so the campaign's history-of-engineering arc shows without the player doing anything; a fixed theme is one setting away.
- **The HUD has its own style, separate from the scene theme.** Players read the era chrome (90s grey bevels on the tutorial, riveted brass, neon glass) as a dated UI rather than a theme, so the default "Modern" interface is one clean HUD over every room: floating frosted-glass islands (`backdrop-filter` blur), 12 to 24 px radii, soft shadows instead of bevels, Nunito in sentence case, one warm accent (orange) plus green for Run and red for Reset, segmented controls and pill chips. The top bar is three islands (back and title, goals, icons), so the room shows between them; the stage inset is measured from the bar instead of hard-coded. It lives entirely in `ui/modern.css` under `html[data-ui='modern']`, so the era chrome in `styles.css` is untouched and stays one setting away ("Classic (match the era)"). `chromeFor` in `core/themes.ts` decides which chrome a theme gets.
- **A secret arcade theme ("Insert Coin") unlocks with the Konami code** (up up down down left right left right B A, anywhere outside a text field). It is an original homage to 8/16-bit cabinets: pixel room, pixelated and posterized parts, an original chiptune style, black pixel-bordered chrome in Press Start 2P and VT323 (both OFL, bundled via Fontsource) and a CRT scanline glass over the whole screen. No names, characters, music or logos from any company are used. The code toggles it on and off; once unlocked it is listed under Theme in Settings and in the sandbox and editor pickers, and it never appears as an era. It always brings its own cabinet chrome whatever the interface style, because a secret theme should transform the whole screen. Pixel fonts are normalised with `font-size-adjust` so every existing px size stays readable.
- **Touch makes fingers reach further instead of making parts bigger.** John wants the game playable on tablets and phones without blowing parts up. The editor tracks whether the last press came from a finger (`EditorController.touch`); while it did, pick and handle reach roughly doubles (knob and grips 24 screen px, corner turn zones 22), the knob sits further out (44 px) so a fingertip on it does not cover the part, and handles draw 1.4x. End grips never reach past 35% of the way to the part's centre, so the middle of a short plank still moves it. Parts, physics and levels are untouched.
- **Two fingers: twist what you hold, otherwise pinch the room.** A second finger landing while the first is dragging the selected part (or one of its handles) turns that part about its centre with the usual angle snap, previewed live and committed as one undo step like a knob drag. Anywhere else the pair pinch-zooms and pans the board and whatever the first finger had started (a box select, a move) is undone. Mouse pan stays on middle/right drag.
- **A part dragged out of the bin by finger rides 44 px above it**, so the finger never hides where it will land; tapping a bin item and then the stage still drops it exactly under the tap. Whether a drop lands on the stage is hit-tested (`elementFromPoint`) rather than read from the event target, because touch events stay with the element first touched (the bin item). Bin items use `touch-action: pan-y`, so an up-down swipe scrolls the bin and a sideways one drags the part out; the stage canvas takes every gesture (`touch-action: none`).
- **On a phone held sideways the HUD lives in the side gutters, not in bars.** A landscape phone (about 19.5:9) is wider than the room (about 16:9), so the room is limited by height and the spare space is at the sides; any top or bottom bar shrinks the room. Below 520 px tall (`ui/touch.css`, and `compactHud()` in PlayScreen for the stage insets) the parts bin is a strip of icons on the left with undo, redo and fit under it, and the right gutter holds a rail (leave, hint, more) with a round Run button at the bottom; during a run, pause, step and rewind stack above it. "More" opens the rest of the top bar as a sheet. Goal chips are dropped because every goal is already tagged in the room, and the part settings open from a wrench on the selection toolbar instead of docking under the bin. The room gets the full screen height. John found the first compact version (shrunken bars) still took too much room.
- **Tablets and small laptops get one slim column of parts.** Under 1280 px wide the room is limited by width, so the bin is one 128 px column instead of two, and on touch screens the turn, flip, copy and delete buttons appear only on the selection toolbar (not also in the bottom bar and the properties panel). A phone held upright gets a "turn your phone sideways" overlay on the play screen rather than a squeezed room; menus still work upright. Tooltips only show for a mouse (on touch they popped up on every tap), the Controls dialog lists gestures on touch devices, and the page sets `viewport-fit=cover` with safe-area insets and no pinch-zoom of the page, which is also what a wrapped App Store build needs.
- **The properties panel lives under the parts bin in the left column.** As a floating panel at the top right it covered machines in levels whose action happens there.

## Audio

- **Music is generated, not sequenced, but it has a form.** A pure random walk got tiresome within minutes, so the conductor plays sections (intro, A, A, B, break, A, short rest) with fixed progressions and 2-bar motifs that return as statement, answer, sequence and cadence. Each pass through the form draws new motifs and progressions and, every second pass, moves key and nudges tempo. Breaks thin out and rests nearly stop, so long sessions breathe.
- **One music style per visual theme, sharing the conductor.** Styles are data (tempo, metre, keys, progressions, motif rhythms) plus a step function choosing instruments, so a new theme is one entry in `styles.ts`. Stone is pentatonic marimba, kalimba, log and hand drums; steam is a 3/4 oom-pah with tuba, calliope and music box; retro is FM bass, square lead with echo and a drum machine; modern keeps the original lo-fi electric-piano bed; comic is brass stabs, march snare rolls, timpani and pizzicato; future is analog pads, an arpeggiator, sub bass and a gentle sidechain pump. All melodies are generated at runtime, none are quoted.
- **Theme changes crossfade (2.5 s) and keep both players scheduling during the fade**; rapid toggling cuts the oldest fading player quickly so at most three are alive.
- **Levels are calibrated by offline rendering.** Each style's trim and each themed stinger's trim was set from OfflineAudioContext renders of the built game (K-weighted loudness, peak, DC, silence and band checks), so switching theme does not jump in loudness.
- **The simulation tags impacts with a body kind** (`domino`, `heavy`, `ball`, `floor`). Materials alone could not tell a domino clack from a plank knock or a bowling ball from a gear; the tag is advisory and costs nothing in the simulation.
- **Rooms are sunny, but the cheer layer is still knocked back.** Windows, bunting, doodles and plants are painted on their own `cheer` layer with less desaturation than props (so they stay cheerful) but still softened and blurred, and each room gets a final daylight grade (brightness, saturation, fresh wall paint). Nothing round, glowing or bulb-like sits mid-wall where it could be read as a part; the porthole in Far Future has no sun for that reason.
- **Celebrations are timed to the stinger and cancelled on rewind.** The big burst waits 0.55 s for the stinger's hit, per-goal sparkles fire only from live goal events, and scrubbing or stepping back cancels any pending burst, so replays never celebrate early or twice. Particles come from the same capped pool, with fewer in reduced-motion mode.

## Measuring fun

- **Fun is measured through proxies, never as one number.** The headless fun audit (`src/analysis/funAudit.ts`) measures what this genre is loved and hated for: spectacle (chain stages, domains), pacing (first reaction, longest lull), agency (share of the chain that needs the player), forgiveness (does the right idea placed roughly still work) and near misses (does a failing attempt get partway). Its 0–100 score is a sorting aid; the flags are the output.
- **Forgiveness is measured the way the editor places things.** A rough hand is a random offset that is then snapped to the 10 px grid, whole turning steps and whole size steps, because that is what a player with default snapping can actually build. It is measured part by part (the rest placed right) so the report names the fiddly part. Gears are left alone because the editor seats them on their neighbour's teeth.
- **The playtest log stays on the device.** `src/telemetry/playtest.ts` records sessions in localStorage only; players export it by hand. No analytics service, so nothing needs consent and nothing leaves the browser.
- **The audit's thresholds are provisional until real playtests exist.** They were set so flags pick out outliers. `npm run fun:playtests` puts audit scores beside player behaviour so the thresholds can be moved toward what people actually did.


## Hosting

- **The built game works offline after one visit.** A small hand-written service worker (`src/sw.js`, filled in by a Vite plugin in `vite.config.ts`) precaches every file of the build. The cache name is a hash of the file list, so each deploy installs a fresh cache and deletes the old one. Pages are fetched network first, so a new deploy shows up on the next load and the cached copy is only used when the network fails; hashed assets come straight from the cache. Cache lookups ignore `Vary`, because servers send `Vary: Origin` and module scripts are requested with an Origin header the precache request did not have. It is only registered in production builds, never on the dev server.
